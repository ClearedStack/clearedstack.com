Everything the tools and guides rest on has been officially released to the public, and all of it is free to read. This page lists where. Go to these sources, not to a summary, when you need to check a result or settle a disagreement.

On a network with no internet connection, none of these links will open. Bring the documents you need across through your organization’s own process.

## Reading a citation

A citation is an address. Once you can read one, you can find the text.

| Citation | How to read it |
| --- | --- |
| FAR 31.205-14 | Federal Acquisition Regulation, Part 31, Subpart 31.2, section 31.205, subsection 14 |
| FAR 52.232-25 | A FAR clause. Clauses and provisions are all in Part 52. The middle number, 232, points to the part that prescribes it: Part 32 |
| DFARS 252.204-7012 | A defense clause. Defense clauses are in Part 252, and their final number starts at 7000 |
| 48 CFR 31.205-14 | The same FAR section, cited by its place in the Code of Federal Regulations. The FAR is Title 48, Chapter 1 |
| 32 CFR Part 170 | Title 32 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Part 170 |
| 13 CFR 121.201 | Title 13, Part 121, section 201 |
| 10 U.S.C. 3014 | Title 10 of the United States Code, section 3014. This is a law passed by Congress, not a regulation |
| NIST SP 800-171 Rev. 2 | A NIST Special Publication, number 800-171, second revision |
| 91 FR 42594 | Volume 91 of the Federal Register, page 42594 |

Laws passed by Congress sit above regulations. Regulations sit above agency guidance. Your contract sits on top of all of them for your own work, because it says which of them apply to you.

| Source | Where | What is there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Federal Acquisition Regulation | [acquisition.gov/browse/index/far](https://www.acquisition.gov/browse/index/far) | The full FAR, searchable, with every clause |
| Defense FAR Supplement | [acquisition.gov/dfars](https://www.acquisition.gov/dfars) | The DFARS and its companion guidance |
| Other agency supplements | [acquisition.gov](https://www.acquisition.gov) | Each agency’s additions to the FAR |
| Code of Federal Regulations | [ecfr.gov](https://www.ecfr.gov) | Every federal regulation, updated daily, with a view of the text as of any past date |
| Federal Register | [federalregister.gov](https://www.federalregister.gov) | Proposed and final rules as they are published, with the reasoning behind them |
| United States Code | [uscode.house.gov](https://uscode.house.gov) | The laws themselves |

<Callout kind="warning" title="The FAR is being rewritten">
Since 2025 the FAR has been going through a full rewrite. For a time there are two texts: the codified regulation, and the overhaul text that agencies have been directed to follow through deviations. Section and clause numbers differ between them, and defense clauses are being renumbered as well. When a number in a tool, a guide or an old document does not match what you find, search by the title of the section or clause, and read the overhaul pages on acquisition.gov to see which text an agency is using.
</Callout>

## Is it current?

A source can be official and still out of date on your screen. Four checks:

1. **Look for the date.** The eCFR shows the date its text is current as of, at the top of each page. FAR pages show the acquisition circular and its effective date. NIST publications carry a publication date and a revision number.
2. **Check for something pending.** A proposed rule in the Federal Register is not yet the rule, but it tells you a change is coming.
3. **Check for a deviation.** Agencies can direct their contracting officers to depart from the published regulation by issuing a class deviation. Defense deviations are published with the DFARS.
4. **Check your contract.** The version of a clause in your contract, identified by its date, is the version that binds you, even after the regulation changes.

To see what a regulation said on an earlier date, the eCFR has a point-in-time view. That is how you check a tool’s figure against the rule as it stood when the tool was written.

## Guidance is not regulation

Agencies publish a great deal of helpful material that is not binding: frequently asked questions, guidebooks, training slides, memos. It is often the clearest explanation available, and it can change without notice. When a tool or a guide relies on guidance instead of a regulation, it should say so. When guidance and the regulation conflict, the regulation governs.

## What does not count as a source

- **A leaked document**, or one marked for limited distribution that can be found online anyway. Public means officially released.
- **A document from behind a login** that you can reach because of your job.
- **A consultant’s summary, a vendor’s white paper or a forum post**, however accurate. They are useful for finding the rule. They are not the rule.
- **A generated answer** from a chat assistant or a search summary. Ask it where the rule is, then go and read the rule.

This project builds only from the first kind of material: officially released regulations, standards and guidance.
