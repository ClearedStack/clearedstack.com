A solicitation is the government’s written request for offers. It can run to hundreds of pages, and most of those pages are not where the decisions are. This guide explains how the document is organized so you can find the dozen facts that tell you whether to bid, and what a winning answer has to contain.

## The kinds of request

Not everything the government posts is a solicitation, and the different kinds are answered differently.

| Name | What it is | What you send back |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Request for information (RFI) or sources sought | Market research. The government is learning who can do the work | A short description of your capability. It is not an offer and cannot win a contract |
| Draft solicitation | An early version, posted for comment | Questions and comments |
| Request for quotation (RFQ) | A request for prices, common for smaller and simpler buys | A quote |
| Request for proposals (RFP) | The full process for negotiated contracts, with instructions and evaluation factors | A proposal, usually in several volumes |
| Invitation for bids (IFB) | Sealed bidding. The award goes on price to a responsive, responsible bidder | A bid |

Federal opportunities above a set dollar value are posted on [SAM.gov](https://sam.gov) under Contract Opportunities. Searching and reading there is free and needs no account. Downloading some attachments does need one.

An RFI is worth answering. The answers help the government decide whether a purchase can be set aside for small businesses, and how to write the requirement.

## The lettered sections

Many solicitations follow the uniform contract format, which divides the document into thirteen lettered sections. Even when a solicitation is laid out differently, people in this field use the letters as shorthand, so they are worth learning once.

| Section | Title | What you find there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Solicitation/contract form | The cover form: solicitation number, issuing office, due date and time |
| B | Supplies or services and prices/costs | The line items you will price |
| C | Description/specifications/statement of work | The work |
| D | Packaging and marking | How deliverables are packed and labeled |
| E | Inspection and acceptance | How and where the government accepts the work |
| F | Deliveries or performance | Dates, period of performance, place |
| G | Contract administration data | Who administers the contract, and how to invoice |
| H | Special contract requirements | Terms particular to this contract: security, key personnel, transitions |
| I | Contract clauses | The standard clauses, mostly by reference |
| J | List of attachments | Everything attached, by title |
| K | Representations, certifications and other statements | Statements you make about your company |
| L | Instructions, conditions and notices to offerors | How to prepare and submit the proposal |
| M | Evaluation factors for award | How the proposal will be judged |

Sections A through J become the contract. Section K is carried into it by reference. Sections L and M are about the competition, and are not part of the contract.

<Callout kind="note" title="The numbering is in motion">
The Federal Acquisition Regulation is being rewritten. For decades the uniform contract format has been at FAR 15.204-1, and it is still there in the codified text. The overhaul text that agencies have been working from since 2025 moves it to a different section and makes it optional. Expect to see both layouts for some time, and find the rule by its title on [acquisition.gov](https://www.acquisition.gov) instead of trusting a section number from memory, including the ones in this guide.
</Callout>

## When the letters are missing

Purchases of commercial products and services often use a shorter layout, built around a standard form and a set of commercial clauses. There may be no Section L or M by name. The same content is still there under plainer headings:

- **Instructions to offerors** is the equivalent of Section L.
- **Evaluation** or **basis for award** is the equivalent of Section M.
- **Statement of work**, **performance work statement** or **statement of objectives** is the equivalent of Section C.
- **Price schedule** is the equivalent of Section B.

Other transaction agreements, which some defense buyers use for prototypes, are not governed by the FAR at all. Their requests follow whatever format the issuing office chooses. Read them without assumptions.

## What to read first

Reading from page one is the slow way. This order gets you to a bid decision fastest:

1. **The cover page.** Due date, time and time zone. How to submit. Who to ask.
2. **Section L.** What you must hand in: volumes, page limits, format, required forms.
3. **Section M.** What the government will score, and what matters most.
4. **Section C.** The work itself. Can you do it?
5. **Section B.** How the price is structured.
6. **Section H.** Anything unusual: clearances, key personnel, special security terms.
7. **Sections I and K.** The clauses and the statements you must make.
8. **Section J attachments.** Often the real detail lives here: the pricing workbook, the labor category descriptions, the data requirements.

## The first-pass list

Before you spend a day on a solicitation, find these facts. Most are on the cover page or in Sections L and M.

- **Deadlines.** When questions are due. When the proposal is due, to the minute, and in which time zone.
- **How to submit.** A portal, email, or something else, and any size limit on files.
- **Who may bid.** Whether the purchase is set aside for small businesses or a specific program, and under which NAICS code and size standard. See [Codes, registration and small business status](/guides/codes-and-small-business/).
- **Contract type.** Fixed price, time and materials, cost reimbursement, or an ordering contract with task orders.
- **Length.** The base period and the option periods.
- **Place of performance.** On a government site, at your own, or remote.
- **Security.** Whether people or facilities need a clearance, and how government information must be protected. See [Protecting government information](/guides/protecting-government-information/).
- **Basis for award.** Whether the government will trade price against quality, or take the lowest-priced proposal that is technically acceptable.
- **Past performance.** How many references, how recent and how similar.
- **Mandatory qualifications.** Certifications, registrations or experience that are pass or fail.

If you fail a pass-or-fail item, nothing else in the proposal matters.

## Clauses incorporated by reference

Section I can list a hundred clauses by number and title alone. Each one is a term of the contract, with the same force as if it were printed in full. The full text is on [acquisition.gov](https://www.acquisition.gov).

The numbers tell you where a clause comes from:

| Pattern | Source |
| --- | --- |
| `52.2xx-xx` | The Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) |
| `252.2xx-7xxx` | The Defense FAR Supplement (DFARS) |
| Other prefixes | Another agency’s supplement |

You do not need to read every clause on the first pass. These are worth finding early, because they change what the work costs:

| Subject | Long-standing clause | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Safeguarding contractor systems | FAR 52.204-21 | Fifteen basic security requirements for any system holding federal contract information |
| Safeguarding defense information | DFARS 252.204-7012 | NIST SP 800-171 and 72-hour incident reporting |
| CMMC | DFARS 252.204-7021 | A required CMMC level |
| Limitations on subcontracting | FAR 52.219-14 | How much of a set-aside contract you must perform yourself |
| Service contract labor standards | FAR 52.222-41 | Minimum wages and benefits for covered service workers |
| Prompt payment | FAR 52.232-25 | When you are paid, and interest if you are not |

Some of these are being renumbered as part of the rewrite described above. Search by the clause title if a number does not match.

## Amendments and questions

A solicitation changes after it is posted. Each change is an amendment, and each one is part of the solicitation.

- **Watch for amendments.** On SAM.gov you can follow an opportunity. An amendment can move the due date, change the work or replace an attachment.
- **Acknowledge every amendment** in the way the solicitation requires. A proposal that ignores an amendment can be rejected.
- **Ask questions in writing, by the deadline, to the person named.** Answers are normally published to every bidder, so do not put anything proprietary in a question.
- **Do not rely on anything said informally.** Only the written solicitation and its amendments count.

A good question points to a page and paragraph, quotes the text and asks one thing.

## Late is late

A proposal that arrives after the exact time stated is late, and with narrow exceptions a late proposal is not considered. Portals slow down in the last hour, and email attachments are held up by size limits and scanners. Plan to submit a day early.

## Decide whether to bid

A proposal costs real time. Before you commit, answer these honestly:

- Are we eligible, and do we pass every pass-or-fail item?
- Can we do the work in Section C with people we have or can hire?
- Do we have past performance that matches what Section M asks for?
- Can we meet the security requirements by the time they apply?
- Does the price structure in Section B leave room to do the work properly?
- Can we produce everything Section L asks for by the due date?

Deciding not to bid is a legitimate result of reading a solicitation well. When the answer is yes, the next guide covers [answering every requirement](/guides/answer-a-solicitation/).
