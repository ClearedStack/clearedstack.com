Discovery is the first stage of the prior-art engine. It searches the federal contracting landscape to find software, spreadsheets, templates, and calculators that already help people do government-contracting paperwork.

The goal is not to copy what others built. The goal is to see what problems people are already solving, find where free and private options do not exist, and schedule independent clean-room implementations built directly from the underlying public regulations.

To see the complete pipeline and evaluation criteria, visit [How we decide](/how-we-decide/).

## The discovery flow

A candidate is any public tool, workbook, or script that helps with federal paperwork. Discovery sweeps run across public repositories, agency websites, and open community suggestions.

1. **Identify candidate**: The discovery engine or human maintainer identifies a tool that addresses a specific paperwork task, such as indirect cost calculations, solicitation shredding, or compliance crosswalks.
2. **Intake boundary check**: Before any link is saved, it is checked against strict boundary rules. It must be accessible on the public web without credentials, payment, or anti-bot bypass.
3. **Normalize URL**: URLs are cleaned to remove marketing tracking parameters (`utm_*`, `ref`), session IDs, and trailing slashes.
4. **Resolve duplicates**: The engine checks whether the tool is already recorded, whether it is an upstream fork of an existing open-source project, or whether it is a white-label wrapper of an engine already indexed.
5. **Mint record**: Once validated as unique or a distinct derivative, the tool receives an identifier (such as `PA-0001`) and moves to Stage 2 (Record) and Stage 3 (Observe).

## Seven source kinds

The discovery engine looks across seven distinct source kinds to ensure broad coverage of federal contracting paperwork:

1. **Government sites**: Official calculators, interactive web forms, and portals hosted on `.gov` or `.mil` domains. These provide authoritative statutory baselines, but are often server-dependent or unavailable on disconnected networks.
2. **Free vendor tools**: Ungated calculators and estimating utilities published by defense contractors, consulting firms, or software vendors as informational resources.
3. **Commercial product public pages**: Public landing pages, documentation, and feature overviews of commercial software suites. Observers read public documentation only; they never sign up or request sales demos.
4. **Open-source projects**: Public repositories hosted on platforms like GitHub or GitLab containing compliance scripts, document parsers, or command-line utilities.
5. **Spreadsheets and templates**: Downloadable `.xlsx`, `.ods`, or hosted spreadsheet workbooks published by agencies, buying commands, or industry practitioners.
6. **Assistance centers**: Public guides, worksheets, and bid templates published by APEX Accelerators (formerly PTACs), Small Business Development Centers (SBDCs), and university procurement institutes.
7. **Community suggestions**: Specific tools and paperwork tasks submitted directly by contractors, developers, and agency staff through GitHub issues or email.

## Duplicate handling

Many tools on the web share code, run under multiple brand names, or mirror upstream repositories. The discovery engine uses a decision branch to classify each candidate accurately:

  caption="How duplicates, forks, and white-labels are handled"
  question="What is the candidate’s relationship to existing records?"
  options={[
    { when: "Same tool at an alternate URL", then: "Alias as mirror", detail: "Attach the alternate URL to the existing PA record without creating a new identifier." },
    { when: "Open-source fork with zero changes", then: "Alias to upstream", detail: "Link directly to the original parent repository and close the duplicate intake." },
    { when: "Open-source fork with new features", then: "Record as derivative fork", detail: "Mint a new PA identifier, link lineage to the parent record, and evaluate independently." },
    { when: "White-label re-skin of a known engine", then: "Alias to primary creator", detail: "Designate the underlying creator as canonical and note the brand as an alias." },
    { when: "New independent candidate", then: "Mint new record", detail: "Assign a fresh PA-XXXX record and queue for public observation." },
  ]}
/>

- **Mirrors**: If an agency moves a calculator from an old URL to a new path, the new URL is recorded as an alias on the original record.
- **Forks**: Open-source repositories that merely duplicate an existing project without changes are aliased to the upstream maintainer. If a fork introduces substantial independent calculations or updates an obsolete standard, it receives its own record linked to the parent.
- **White-labels**: In government contracting, consulting firms frequently re-skin an identical underlying spreadsheet or calculation engine. The discovery engine identifies shared mathematical models and assets, designating the original developer as primary and aliasing re-branded variants.

## Community suggestions

Community suggestions are a vital discovery vector. When a contractor or agency specialist finds a useful spreadsheet or struggles with a paperwork task, they can suggest it through a GitHub issue.

Suggestions enter the exact same pipeline as automated sweeps. They do not skip screening, scoring, or owner review.

## What we never do

Discovery is passive and public. To preserve project ethics and legal compliance, we never:

- Create user accounts, fill out registration forms, or submit contact information to access tools.
- Sign up for product trials, request sales demonstrations, or negotiate pilot agreements.
- Circumvent paywalls, subscription requirements, access gates, or session limits.
- Solve, bypass, or script around CAPTCHAs, Turnstiles, or anti-bot challenges.
- Ignore or violate `robots.txt` directives or site terms of use regarding automated indexing.
- Submit real company rates, actual proposal text, Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI), or proprietary bid figures into any online form.
- Execute vulnerability scans, penetration testing, fuzzing, or high-volume crawling.

If a tool cannot be inspected from its public pages and documentation without accepting restrictive terms or logging in, discovery records only the public claims and stops.
