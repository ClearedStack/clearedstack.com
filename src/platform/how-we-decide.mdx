Section M of a solicitation tells bidders how their proposals will be judged. This page is the same thing for ideas: the complete set of rules by which a candidate tool is screened, scored and decided. The rules are public so that anyone can see why one tool was built and another was not.

The screening and scoring are done by fixed rules with no AI, so the same facts always give the same score. The decision is made by a person.

## The five knockout gates

Before any points are counted, a candidate has to pass five yes-or-no tests. Failing one ends the evaluation, and the reason is recorded.

1. **Public source.** Its rules, formulas and thresholds come from officially released material: statutes, the FAR, the DFARS, NIST publications. Not a paywalled manual, and not industry folklore.
2. **Runs in the browser.** It can work entirely on the user’s machine, with no server and no outside service.
3. **Private by design.** It works offline, needs no account, tracks nothing and can enforce a content security policy that blocks outside requests.
4. **One job.** It does a single task that can be described in one sentence starting with a verb. A suite fails, unless it is split into smaller ideas.
5. **Arithmetic, not advice.** It computes, structures or looks things up. It does not give legal advice or stand in for a contracting officer’s judgment.

## The 100-point rubric

A candidate that passes the gates is rated from 1 to 5 on seven factors. Each rating is multiplied by the factor’s weight, giving a total between 20 and 100.

| Factor | Weight | A rating of 1 | A rating of 3 | A rating of 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Need for small businesses | ×4, up to 20 | A rare edge case | Comes up now and then, such as once a year | A recurring obstacle for most small contractors, costly when done wrong |
| Gap: no free, private option exists | ×4, up to 20 | Good free tools that run on the user’s machine already exist | A clumsy government portal, or open tools that are hard to use | Only paid products or lead-capture forms exist |
| Strength of the public source | ×3, up to 15 | An informal slide deck or an unclear draft | A policy memo or an interim rule | A statute, a codified regulation or a published standard |
| Fits in a browser | ×3, up to 15 | Needs very large data or heavy computing | Needs moderate bundled tables or a special parser | Simple arithmetic or text handling with very little data |
| Small enough to read | ×2, up to 10 | A sprawling program with many settings | Moderate, with clear inputs and outputs | Short enough to read in one sitting |
| Can be checked | ×2, up to 10 | Rests on interpretation | A formula with edge cases that need warnings | Plain arithmetic with official worked examples to test against |
| Low upkeep | ×2, up to 10 | Needs frequent data updates or fragile page reading | Updated once a year | Rests on text that changes only when the rule is amended |

## What the score recommends

| Result | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| 75 or more, and a Gap rating above 1 | **Build** it from the public rule |
| 75 or more, and a Gap rating of 1 | **Link** to the existing free, private tool instead of rebuilding it |
| 55 to 74 | **Park** it: a sound idea held back by missing data, a pending rule or capacity |
| Below 55 | **Reject** it, with the breakdown of points published |

One more rule: a candidate rated 4 or 5 on Need and 1 or 2 on Small enough to read is flagged for the owner to consider splitting into smaller tools.

ClearedStack does not exist to duplicate good work. The Link outcome is there so that a tool which is already free, private and built on public sources gets pointed to, not rebuilt.

## Breaking a tie

When two candidates have the same total, they are ordered by these tests, in this order:

1. **Fit with the current phase** of the roadmap.
2. **A higher Gap rating.**
3. **A higher Low upkeep rating.**
4. **A higher Small enough to read rating.**
5. **More independent requests** from the community.

## The decision is a person’s

The score is a recommendation. The owner reviews each scored candidate and chooses one of the outcomes: build, link, merge into another tool, park or reject. The owner can also override the recommendation, or reopen a candidate that was screened out. An override needs a written reason, and the reason is recorded in the public decision log.

## Where the rules come from

Each area of the catalog has its own profile of thresholds for the rubric: pricing, proposals, cyber, market research and DevSecOps. A candidate in an area with no profile is not scored by guesswork. It is paused until a profile is written, the same way any worker handles a case it has no rule for. See [The pipeline and its ten workers](/platform/pipeline/#sub-tools-one-rule-for-each-case).

To suggest a tool, see [Request a tool or add your own](/guides/request-or-add-a-tool/). How often a tool is asked for is the fifth tie-breaker above.
